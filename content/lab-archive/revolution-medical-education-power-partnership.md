I taught a course at IMC Krems University of Applied Sciences, in the middle of Austrian wine country, that broke from how medical education usually runs. It covered three things most curricula treat as separate: the patient narrative, provider resilience, and the practical mechanics of partnership between the two.

The patient narrative half is simple to state and hard to institutionalize: a diagnosis is not the same thing as a life, and future clinicians need to hear the difference directly from the people living it, not summarized in a case study. Students who hear the actual arc, symptom onset, the fear before diagnosis, the grind of treatment, come away seeing the chart differently.

The provider half exists because resilience isn't optional in this work, it's survival infrastructure. Burnout and compassion fatigue are not personal failings. They're predictable outcomes of the job, and a curriculum that ignores them is training clinicians for a version of medicine that doesn't account for the actual cost of practicing it.

The partnership half is where the two meet: shared decision-making, experience mapping a patient's actual journey through the system, and co-design that puts patients in the room when services are built, not just when they're being asked to comply with what was decided without them. Role-play made these concrete instead of theoretical: it's easier to bring a patient's voice into a shift when you've practiced it before the real shift arrives.

The course worked. I'd like to see more medical schools run something like it, not as an elective add-on but as core training for anyone who's going to spend a career on the other side of the exam table from people like me.

My path back didn't start with confident strides. It started with rubber bands and stubbornness.

I'd been bedridden. The healthcare system had already given up on me. So I became my own rehabilitation specialist, because nobody else was going to do it.

The start was brutal. Every attempt at movement brought fainting, or the near-miss version of it: crushing chest pain and the feeling that something terrible was about to happen. Every failure threatened to snuff out whatever hope I had left.

I had no rehab center. No professional guidance. No equipment. Just willpower and a pair of rubber bands, smuggled to me by a physiotherapist who cared more than the system did. My parents' home became my recovery ward. I planned every movement around the constant risk of fainting.

Those rubber bands became my lifeline. I gave up on the fantasy of waking up healed one morning. Years of muscle loss meant the simplest movement was a monumental one. I started with two or three reps, most of which ended in fainting. Three weeks later I was doing hundreds a day.

Every pull against those yellow bands was aimed at something. The healthcare system that abandoned me. The people who didn't believe me. The girl who told me she "wouldn't date a sick man." None of that broke me.

The harder part wasn't physical. Professionals had called me a lost cause. I had to relearn patience and hold onto one fact: this was my life, and I was the only one who could save it.

Here's what I actually learned: we're bound only by what we believe is possible, sick or not. Thalidomide babies did things doctors said they couldn't. I could choose to manage my symptoms instead of letting them manage me.

Rubber bands. Then standing. Then walking. Each one was a battle I won.

Tomorrow doesn't come for free. You seize it, or you don't.

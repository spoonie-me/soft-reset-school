As an entrepreneur living with chronic pain, I've watched the same mistake happen over and over: employers read limitation as weakness, when it's usually the thing that built the exact skills they're looking for.

People who navigate daily adversity learn to adapt out of necessity, not inspiration. Creative problem-solving under real constraint. Resilience that wasn't optional. A focus that had to survive obstacles most colleagues never face. That's not a metaphor for grit. It's a trained skill, built the hard way, and most employers never think to ask whether a candidate has it.

The stereotype does real damage. It leaves a pool of talent sitting unrecognized because nobody asked who actually understands accessibility better than the people who've had to navigate barriers themselves, or who embodies resilience more than people for whom adversity was never optional.

Disability inclusion is also, plainly, good business: companies that do it well see measurably better outcomes on revenue and retention. That shouldn't be the reason to do it. It's a reason it's an easy decision anyway.

Judge people by capability, not by assumed deficit. Extend the opportunity, and you're not just changing one person's situation. You're getting access to a kind of problem-solving your organization has probably never had.

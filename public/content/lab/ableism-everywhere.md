Ableism is systemic discrimination that favors able-bodied people, built on the belief that disability makes someone lesser. Living with multiple invisible chronic illnesses, I run into it constantly, made worse by the fact that nothing about how I look signals what I'm dealing with.

It shows up in ways that are easy to miss if you're not the one absorbing them: buildings and processes that were never designed with disability in mind, the quiet assumption that able-bodied experience is the default, language and portrayals that treat disability as tragedy, and a persistent belief that disability needs to be overcome rather than accommodated.

Concretely, for me: a yoga instructor who refused to modify poses for my chronic pain and fatigue. Train conductors who demanded I stand up while I was in the middle of a dizzy spell that made standing dangerous. Being read as unintelligent when a processing delay was actually my disability, not my capability. Being accused outright of faking symptoms that are real and largely invisible.

None of that is disability being disappointing. Disability is a natural form of human variation. It becomes a disadvantage specifically when a system chooses not to accommodate it, which is a choice, not a law of nature.

What actually changes this: education on ableist language and systems, more disabled voices in the actual conversation instead of being talked about, real accommodation instead of token gestures, and policy that backs disability rights rather than treating them as optional generosity.

Further reading: [Ableism 101](https://www.healthline.com/health/ableism), [Disability Visibility](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/611478/disability-visibility-by-alice-wong/) by Alice Wong, and [The Disability Rights Movement: From Charity to Confrontation](http://credo.library.umass.edu/view/full/mupui101340037) by Doris Zames Fleischer and Frieda Zames.

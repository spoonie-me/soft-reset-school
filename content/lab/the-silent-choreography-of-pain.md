I remember the exact moment I learned the rule. Standing in a fluorescent-lit office, my body inflamed, a colleague asked the perfunctory "how are you?" The expected answer was "fine, thanks." I felt the fork in the road: truth or performance. I chose performance. "I'm good, just a little tired." Relief crossed her face. The social order stayed intact. I'd passed another test of belonging.

That test runs constantly for anyone with chronic illness or invisible disability. Before every social engagement there's a private calculation: how much energy do I actually have, which symptoms can I hide, which medications can I take without anyone noticing. It becomes background processing, running underneath every conversation, meeting, and moment of parenting.

The masking is physical too: controlling your face when pain spikes, timing your breath to manage dizziness quietly, positioning yourself near an exit before anyone knows you'll need one. You learn the bathroom locations in every building you frequent, because that's the only place the mask can come off for thirty seconds.

We've built a specialized vocabulary for this. "A bit under the weather" means I can barely stand. "Just need a minute" means my vision is tunneling. Say the true version instead, and most people don't know what to do with it. They minimize it, or try to fix it, or compare it to something smaller. Few people know how to just witness it: "that sounds incredibly difficult, I see you carrying this."

The research backs up what this actually costs. Suppressing authentic expression is linked to measurably worse pain, depression, and anxiety. The mask built to protect your place in the world becomes its own source of harm.

The alternative, full disclosure, carries its own risk: jobs lost, relationships strained, an identity reduced to a diagnosis. The world has limited patience for a condition that doesn't resolve into a tidy arc of overcoming.

What actually helps: a small circle who get the unfiltered version, so the performance isn't required constantly. A way to state a boundary as a preference rather than a confession. Scheduling recovery time with the same seriousness as the event itself. Counting the energy spent on masking as real energy spent, because it is.

None of this makes the mask unnecessary. It means the mask doesn't have to be the whole identity. There's a real courage in showing up at all, masked or not, and claiming space anyway.

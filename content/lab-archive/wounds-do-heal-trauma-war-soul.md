The events of the past weeks have reached communities from the Gaza border to Tel Aviv, and every life lost has left a mark far beyond the immediate loss. Children are sleeping with one eye open. People who lived through decades of prior conflict are shaking their heads at a scale of violence that still finds new ways to shock them. Even people who weren't physically near any of it are carrying real emotional weight.

Trauma doesn't check age, gender, or geography before it lands. It can build slowly or arrive all at once, and the hypervigilance and despair that follow it are heavy enough on their own to push someone already struggling further down.

Even here, something holds. Families and communities are pulling closer together. Strangers are showing up for each other in ways that matter. That's not a resolution to what happened. It's the thing that makes it survivable while the actual work of healing still has to happen.

That work is concrete, not abstract: real, accessible mental health care for the soldiers carrying this home, and for the civilians who lived through it too. Space made deliberately for grief and anger, not rushed past because there's no time for it. None of that happens quickly. It happens through people actually listening to each other and validating what the other person went through, instead of comparing wounds.

PTSD, in soldiers and civilians alike, is evidence that this kind of trauma doesn't just pass through people and leave. It changes what's there afterward. The only way through it that I've ever seen work is treating both the trauma and the resilience it eventually reveals as real, and giving people the actual support, not just the encouragement, to rebuild.

Mental health still gets treated as medicine's black sheep, even now, with a far better understanding of the neural pathophysiology behind it and a depression and burnout crisis that's frankly epidemic.

It's time mental health access looked like primary care access: a mental health professional embedded in every department, for patients and for the staff treating them, not a separate system you have to fight your way into. It's time healthcare shifted toward prevention instead of treating crises that were largely predictable.

Here's the admission underneath all of it: I've never met a chronically ill or acutely ill patient with no emotional involvement in their condition. That means I have a mental illness too. I'm not hiding that anymore. I own it, and I advocate from inside it, not from some healthier vantage point above it.

The stigma is the actual barrier. People stay quiet out of fear of being judged, labeled, or losing a job over it, when a mental health condition is exactly as real as a physical one. Breaking that requires people actually talking about it, not just agreeing in the abstract that stigma is bad.

Access is the second barrier: long waits, high costs, care that's theoretically available and practically out of reach for most people who need it.

None of this resolves without treating health holistically, physical, mental, social, together, instead of routing mental health to a separate, chronically underfunded lane. That's a system problem before it's an awareness problem.

I'm a chronic illness advocate and a mental health advocate, because for me those were never two separate things. I'm asking you to stop treating them as separate too.

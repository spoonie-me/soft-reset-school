Most wearables don't fail on the sensors. They fail on the body they're strapped to.

I wore the Fitbit Air. It didn't innovate. It made me uncomfortable. And the price tells you who it was built for.

## What it actually is

The Air is a screenless band. A small pod that reads your heart rate, blood oxygen, temperature and movement, then hands all of it to the Google Health app. $99.99. About five grams. Up to a week of battery.

One reviewer calls it a Whoop competitor for the masses. That's the whole problem.

Screenless bands are not new. Whoop built that lane years ago. Google walked into it with a lower price and a bigger audience.

## Credit where it's due

The Air is light. It's cheap for what it is. One reviewer wore it to bed and, for the first time with any wearable, didn't fight it all night.

That's a real step. It points the right way.

My wrist didn't agree. For me it was uncomfortable. I felt it. And I don't keep wearing what I can feel.

## The price on the box and the price after

$99.99 sounds like a gift.

Then there's the coach. Health Coach, the AI feature Google leads with, is a subscription. $10 a month. $100 a year. Three months free.

Run the numbers. Keep the coach and you pay about $200 in year one. About $300 by year two. About $400 by year three.

![What each one costs you, cumulative: Fitbit Air with Health Coach against a RingConn Gen 2](/content/lab/img/fitbit-air-vs-ringconn-cost.svg)

A RingConn Gen 2 lists at $299, once. No subscription. The app is free. Mine does everything the Air does.

Year one, the band wins. Year two, they tie. Year three, the ring is a hundred dollars ahead and the gap keeps growing.

## The coach that invented a run

I spent nine years as a paramedic. I know what a wrong number costs.

A reviewer at 9to5Google caught the coach inventing a six-mile run he never ran. It forgot workouts he had logged. It called his heart rate steady during efforts that were anything but.

Steady. That word is the whole issue for me.

I have POTS. My heart rate doubles when I stand up. [Thirty-three doctors](/lab/thirty-three-doctors) told me nothing was wrong while that was happening. The variation is the diagnosis. Flatten it and you delete the only thing I bought a sensor to see.

![Illustration of a heart rate near 60 lying down, jumping to about 120 on standing, with a dashed average line that hides the jump](/content/lab/img/pots-heart-rate-standing.svg)

A guessing coach is fine for a jogger. For chronic illness it's noise with a confident voice.

Sounding sure before being right is the oldest failure in medicine. Now it has an app.

## No screen, no answer

The Air has no display. To see my heart rate I open a phone app.

When I stand and the room tilts, I don't want to find my phone. I want the number on my wrist. Now.

Forgettable is good. Silent is not.

## The score that grades you

The Air leans on a readiness score. The same reviewer said it never felt accurate. I believe it.

I've watched a score judge my worst days. On a crash day that number isn't information. It's a verdict. Closed rings, open rings, a red bar that says you failed at resting.

I don't need a wearable to grade my body. I need it to record what my body did and stay quiet.

## Who the step is for

Every company wants more health data. It's the most valuable thing a body produces.

Google has the app, the ecosystem, and now a low-priced sensor to put on a lot of wrists. I understand the move. It's smart.

Look at the incentives. Google's is more health data. Mine is fewer flares. Sometimes those point the same way. The Air reads to me like a product where they don't. More data for Google first. Better days for me somewhere down the list.

I want my heart rate to belong to me. I wrote about what that looks like when your whole medical record [lives on a machine you own](/lab/my-medical-record-lives-on-a-gb10-now).

## What I built instead

I got tired of waiting for someone to build this for people like me.

I'd been here before. I bought a £600 Apple Watch Ultra to understand my POTS. The data was interesting for a while. Then I noticed I was spending as much time managing the watch as I was getting value from it. Charging. Updating. Being careful with it because £600 is £600.

So I flashed my own firmware onto an open-source PineTime and built a POTS watchface. Orthostatic heart rate detection. An HRV proxy. Activity rings for dysautonomia. I own every line. [I wrote about the $26 watch here](/lab/pine64-smartwatch).

It isn't the holy grail either. It sits on a wrist, and my wrist hurts.

## The holy grail

I'm still looking. A ring or a sensor I never feel. A watch so light and small my wrist doesn't ache by noon.

The RingConn gets closest so far. Not there yet.

Here's the test I run on everything.

If I can feel it, I take it off. If I take it off, it measures nothing.

![The comfort test: I can feel it, I take it off, it measures nothing](/content/lab/img/the-comfort-test.svg)

For a body like mine, comfort is the spec. Everything else is a number on a box.

Real innovation in wearables is a sensor a chronically ill person forgets is there and still trusts. Nobody has shipped that yet.

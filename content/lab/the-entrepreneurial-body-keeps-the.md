Every entrepreneur with a chronic illness knows this moment: mid-pitch, adrenaline masking the pain, performing confidence while the body is screaming to stop. An investor asks about the five-year vision, and you're genuinely unsure whether your body will cooperate for the next five hours.

Startup culture doesn't talk about this. It talks about crushing it and grinding harder.

This Rosh Hashanah marked something different for me: choosing honesty over the carefully filtered founder persona I'd been curating for years, not just for my own sake but for the other founders navigating the same intersection of chronic illness and building a business.

LinkedIn runs on a theater of invincibility: the 4am wake-ups, the back-to-back meetings, the "sleep when you're dead" posture that could genuinely kill some of us. I've been complicit in that performance myself, posting the wins and hiding the flare-ups, talking about pushing through when what I actually needed was to stop.

Becoming a father broke that performance open. You can't perform invincibility holding a newborn at 3am while your body is in pain, and the same vulnerability that supposedly makes you weak as an entrepreneur is exactly what makes you present as a parent.

Investors tend to read a health challenge as business risk, conflating physical limitation with entrepreneurial capacity. What that framing misses: managing a chronic illness every day is a working masterclass in resource allocation, adaptive problem-solving, and sustainability planning. When your energy is genuinely limited, vanity metrics stop being affordable. You're forced to ask what actually moves the business forward instead of what just feels like progress.

The illness made me a systems thinker before I had the term for it. It taught me to build things that function without my constant intervention, because some days intervention isn't possible, and it turned delegation from a leadership best practice into an actual survival requirement. When every meeting has a real physical cost, you get exceptionally good at deciding which ones are worth having at all.

This year I let go of a few things on purpose: the performance of constant availability, the shame around needing accommodations, and the myth of the self-made founder, which chronic illness makes impossible to believe in the first place, because nobody gets through this alone.

What I'd want from the ecosystem instead: funding models that read years of navigating medical complexity as a real crisis-management skill, not a red flag. Success metrics that include sustainability and founder wellbeing alongside growth. A culture where "I'm working from home today" is a strategic choice, not a confession.

If you're building something while managing a chronic condition: the experience isn't a liability you're overcoming. It's a perspective that makes the thing you build harder to replace. The days you prioritize treatment aren't days you weren't working. They're what keeps the business viable at all.

Shanah Tovah. Here's to a year where fewer of us are hiding the parts that are actually true.

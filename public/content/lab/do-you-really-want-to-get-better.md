Here's a question chronically ill people rarely say out loud, even to themselves: do I actually want to get better?

Asking it feels like betrayal, like admitting the suffering was optional. It isn't. Nobody chooses chronic illness and nobody can simply decide their way out of the biology of it. But underneath the desire for relief, something more complicated is usually also true.

A diagnosis, once it finally arrives, doesn't just explain the symptoms. It builds a scaffolding: a community, a vocabulary, a legitimate reason to say no to things that used to be unrefusable. "I am chronically fatigued" stops being a description and becomes an identity. Recovery threatens to take the symptoms away and the entire structure built around them along with it.

That's not secondary gain, and it's not weakness. For someone who never learned how to say no, whose limits were never respected when stated plainly, illness can become the only language the body has left that actually gets heard. Taking that away without replacing it with something else is not healing. It's demolition.

I've watched people get close to real improvement and then quietly sabotage it, not from a lack of desire to feel better, but from something closer to instinct: the system they built around the illness is the only structure they have, and dismantling it feels like falling with nothing underneath.

The more useful question isn't "why don't I want to get better." It's "what happens to me if I do." Answer that honestly first, build the replacement structure, and the ambivalence usually loosens on its own.

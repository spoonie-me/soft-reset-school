I built an entire speaking career on a story with a clean shape: fell ill, nearly lost everything, fought back, recovered, now here to tell you what I learned. Audiences believed it. I mostly believed it too.

Then I relapsed. Mid-career, in front of the same audiences who'd booked the recovered version of me. The fatigue, the pain, the dizziness came back with the same intensity as before, and I had to stand on stages while it was happening, maintaining a version of strength I didn't currently have.

The obvious question followed me off every one of those stages: what right did I have to tell people to rise above their circumstances when I was, again, exactly inside mine?

The answer wasn't in finding my way back to the clean recovery story. It was in admitting the story had never been accurate. Resilience was never about the absence of suffering, or about being fixed. It's the capacity to face the bad stretch honestly, again, without needing it to resolve into a lesson on schedule.

So I started saying the true version from the stage instead: that I'd relapsed, that self-doubt came with it, that self-compassion mattered more than the performance of having it together. That was a harder story to deliver. It was also the first time in years the message actually matched the person delivering it.

The body can betray the plan. The willingness to say so out loud, instead of performing past it, is the only part of the message that was ever actually true.

Nurses buried in data entry. Doctors spending half their time transcribing instead of treating. Patients repeating their own medical history at every new login because nothing talks to anything else. Everyone in the system wants the same thing: software that works. What we have instead is a market of vendors each building a proprietary silo and calling it innovation.

The fix isn't another app. It's shared infrastructure: open standards, secure cross-organizational data access, a system where a patient's record follows them instead of resetting at every door. This already exists, in pieces, in more than one country. Israel's OFEK system, Austria's ELGA, and Estonia's X-Road all show that secure, interoperable health data exchange is a solved technical problem. It just hasn't been adopted as the default everywhere it should be.

OpenEHR is worth naming specifically, because it does something most systems don't: it lets patients add their own experience and observations alongside the clinical record, rather than treating the chart as the doctor's document that the patient happens to be about. That's what patient empowerment actually looks like in a database schema, not just in a mission statement.

None of this works without trust, which means security has to be real, not decorative. A tamper-evident record of who accessed what, when, does more for patient trust than any privacy policy nobody reads.

The technology isn't the hard part anymore. Estonia, Israel, and Austria have already proven the model works. What's missing is the will to stop building competing silos and adopt what's already been shown to work: one shared standard, built with doctors, patients, and developers actually in the same room, instead of guessing at what the other two need.
